Seiunji栖 雲 寺

The garden is not in front of the temple. The garden is the temple.

A temple made of stone. The garden is not an object placed in front of the hall — the garden is the temple, a slope of granite boulders at 1,050 metres where monks have sat since the fourteenth century. People still sit on those rocks. That is the whole point of the place.

What Seiunji is

Founded 1348

Tenmokusan Seiunji 天目山栖雲寺 is a temple of the Rinzai school of Zen, Kenchōji branch (臨済宗建長寺派), founded in 1348 by the monk Gōkai Honjō 業海本浄.

Gōkai crossed to Yuan-dynasty China in 1318 and studied under Zhongfeng Mingben 中峰明本 on Mt. Tianmu 天目山, near Hangzhou. He returned to Japan in 1326. Where he went for the next twenty-two years is unknown. In 1348 he settled in this valley in Yamanashi, which resembled the Tianmu he had left behind, and named the mountain after it.

He did not build a monastery and then add a garden. He took the mountainside itself — the boulders, the caves, the trees — as the training hall. The forms of sitting practised here are named for that: rock zazen (岩坐禅), cave zazen (岩窟坐禅), and sitting beneath the trees (樹下坐禅).

Kyoto and Kamakura hold some of the greatest Zen architecture in the world, and they are worth every hour you give them. Seiunji offers something different and much smaller: a temple where the practice, not the building, is the thing on display — and where you are expected to join it rather than photograph it.

What you can do here

Five ways to visit

Visit the stone garden

Open every day, 8:00 to 17:00. About thirty minutes to walk. ¥300 in the honour box at the entrance. No booking, no ticket desk, usually no one there but you.

Visiting →

Sit zazen坐禅

A monthly sitting on the last Sunday of each month, open to beginners, no reservation needed. Longer Genjū retreats 幻住坐禅会 of one or two days, several times a year, limited to fifteen people.

Zazen & retreats →

Stay overnight

Lodging inside the temple — eleven places, shared sleeping space separated by gender, bring your own sleeping bag — or temple camping in the grounds, one group per night, firewood included.

Staying →

See the treasures

A seated statue of Zhongfeng Mingben designated an Important Cultural Property of Japan; the iron war fan of Takeda Shingen; and a Yuan-dynasty painting on silk of a figure holding a cross, designated by Yamanashi Prefecture as a Manichaean image.

Treasures & history →

Book a retreat or an experience

Zazen, sutra copying, temple cleaning, soba making, a meal taken in the monastic form. Sessions from sixty minutes, up to twenty people. Reservation required.

Three things visitors usually do not expect

Before you decide to come

1

It is a working training temple, not a museum.

Nothing here is staged for visitors. On most days the stone garden is unattended; you pay into a box and walk in.

On retreat days, guests join the same schedule as everyone else — including samu 作務, the work practice, which in plain terms means cleaning floors.

2

The mountain has a history of violence.

Seiunji is the memorial temple of the Takeda clan. In 1582, with Oda forces closing in, the last lord Takeda Katsuyori fled toward Tenmokuzan and took his own life at Tano, ending the clan. By the temple’s own tradition he was making for Seiunji and turned back because it had already been burned — a detail the period chronicles do not record.

The kitchen-quarters (kuri 庫裏) standing today were rebuilt in 1592 with land granted by Tokugawa Ieyasu.

3

According to temple tradition, this is where soba noodles began.

The temple claims to be the birthplace of soba-kiri 蕎麦切り — cut buckwheat noodles — on the strength of a passage in Shiojiri 塩尻 by the Owari retainer Amano Sadakage, and a tradition that Gōkai brought the technique back from Hangzhou.

It is a tradition, not a settled historical fact, and we present it as such. The soba festival each November is real enough.

The mountain through the year

Spring · Summer · Autumn · Winter

春Spring

The weeping cherry comes out above the stone Kannon. The valley is a fortnight behind Tokyo, so the blossom is late here.

What happensIn March the temple sows buckwheat and the priest cuts ten-parts soba. The Marishiten festival falls on a Sunday in mid-April, when the shrine is opened.

夏Summer

Moss closes over the granite and the whole slope goes green. At 1,050 metres it stays cool when the plain below does not — this is the best season to sit outside for an hour.

What happensA tea gathering in the green shade at the end of May, and another on the first Sunday of June. The founder is remembered on 27 July.

秋Autumn

Maples turn against Mt. Fuji, which stands clear of the ridge on cold mornings. The gorge trail from the station is at its best.

What happens7–8 November: the soba festival, and the airing of the treasures — the one occasion in the year the temple’s treasures are shown. The Manichaean goshuin is issued on those two days only.

冬Winter

Snow lies on the boulders and the slope goes quiet. Sitting does not stop. Very few people come, which is the argument for coming.

What happensZazen at Daibosatsu Pass in December. Note that the seasonal mountain bus does not run from mid-December to mid-April — use the city bus, which runs all year.

The garden is open every day of the year.
If you are free to choose when to come, choose a quiet month.

Treasures & history

Seiunji holds one object that has no straightforward parallel: a Yuan-dynasty Chinese painting on silk, designated a cultural property of Yamanashi Prefecture, in which Buddhist, Manichaean and Christian imagery appear together. This page sets out what is documented about it, and — equally important for anyone citing this page — what is not.

The painting of a figure holding a cross 絹本著色十字架捧持マニ像

Japanese designation name絹本著色十字架捧持マニ像　一幅 (Kenpon-chakushoku Jūjika-hōji Mani-zō, ippuku)
StatusDesignated Tangible Cultural Property of Yamanashi Prefecture (painting), 11 July 2013
MediumHanging scroll; ink and colour on silk, with extensive use of gold pigment. It is a painting, not a sculpture.
Dimensions153.3 × 58.7 cm
DateYuan dynasty (13th–14th century) in the Japanese designation. Gulácsi (2009) proposes twelfth or thirteenth century.
OwnerSeiunji
LocationOn deposit at the Yamanashi Prefectural Museum, Fuefuki City. It is not normally at the temple.

What is depicted

A single figure seated on a multi-tiered hexagonal lotus throne, with a head nimbus and a body halo, beneath a canopy. Hair falls in several strands over each shoulder. Over a pale violet inner robe the figure wears a white outer robe edged in gold and red. Four square cartouches — at both sides of the robe and at both knees — each contain the head of a crowned figure. The right hand is raised in a gesture resembling abhaya; the left hand holds a cross.

How it came to be called Manichaean

The box inscription reads Kokūzō Bosatsu 虚空蔵菩薩 (Ākāśagarbha), and for a long time the painting was transmitted under that name.

2006Izumi Takeo 泉武夫, “景教聖像の可能性 ─栖雲寺藏傳虚空藏畫像について”, Kokka 國華 1330, pp. 3–17 — proposes a Nestorian Christian (Jingjiao) icon.
2009Zsuzsanna Gulácsi, “A Manichaean ‘Portrait of the Buddha Jesus’: Identifying a Twelfth- or Thirteenth-century Chinese Painting from the Collection of Seiun-ji Zen Temple”, Artibus Asiae 69/1, pp. 91–144 — identifies it as a Manichaean image of Jesus. This is the principal English-language study.
2010Moriyasu Takao 森安孝夫, “日本に現存するマニ教絵画の発見とその歴史的背景”, Inner Asian Studies 内陸アジア史研究 25, pp. 1–29.
2015Izumi Takeo, “栖雲寺画像をめぐって”, in Yoshida Yutaka & Furukawa Shōichi (eds.), 中国江南マニ教絵画研究, Rinsen Shoten, pp. 183–195.

The 2013 prefectural designation adopted the name Mani-zō following this scholarship. Note, however, that the prefecture’s own description keeps both readings, describing a “Mani figure holding a cross of the Greek-cross form (Nestorian Christianity)”. The administration has not settled on a single reading, and neither do we.

In English the painting is often cited as the ”Manichaean Painting of the Buddha Jesus” (Chinese Yishu fo zhen 夷數佛幀). That title follows Gulácsi’s identification; it is not a translation of the Japanese designation.

Provenance

The prefectural and national records set out two accounts and do not choose between them:

  1. Brought back from Yuan China by the founder Gōkai Honjō, who was in China from 1318 to 1326.
  2. Owned by the Christian daimyo Arima Harunobu 有馬晴信, who is said to have acquired it in Kyushu as an image of Christ and to have brought it with him when he was exiled to Kai province in 1612.

What this page does not claim

We set these limits deliberately, so that this page can be cited without being corrected.

  • We do not claim a documented chain of transmission. Both accounts above are transmitted accounts, not established fact.
  • We do not claim that the identification “Manichaean” is universally accepted. It is the name under which the work is designated in Japan, and the prefecture’s own wording preserves the Nestorian reading alongside it.
  • We do not claim the painting is unique in the world — only that comparable cases are very few. Its re-identification is reported to have prompted the recognition of further Manichaean paintings in Japan.

Seeing it

The treasure hall is currently closed and this figure is not on general display. There are two points of access:

  • A special goshuin 御朱印 of the Manichaean figure is issued on two days only in November, as a double-page spread, for an offering of ¥800.
  • During the soba festival (see below), the temple’s treasures are aired and displayed (hōmotsu kazeire 宝物風入れ展), and the head priest gives a talk on the treasures daily from 12:00 to 12:30.

To enquire about viewing a cultural property outside those occasions, contact the temple in advance.

History

1318 — Gōkai Honjō crosses to Yuan China.
1326 — He returns to Japan, having studied under Zhongfeng Mingben on Mt. Tianmu near Hangzhou. His movements for the next twenty-two years are unknown.
1348 (Jōwa 4) — He founds Seiunji in a Yamanashi valley whose landscape resembles the Tianmu he had left, and names the mountain Tenmokusan 天目山 accordingly. He does not build a monastery around a garden; he takes the granite slope itself as the training ground.

Takeda Nobumitsu 武田信満, thirteenth head of the Takeda clan and six generations before Takeda Shingen, took his own life on Mt. Tenmoku. Seiunji became the clan’s memorial temple thereafter.

1582 — Takeda Katsuyori 武田勝頼, the last head of the clan, is said by temple tradition to have made for Seiunji in his final retreat. Oda forces reach the temple ahead of him and burn it to the ground. Katsuyori turns back and takes his own life. The Takeda clan ends.

1592 — With land granted by Tokugawa Ieyasu, the kuri 庫裏 is rebuilt. It still stands, more than four centuries later, and is a Yamanashi Prefecture designated cultural property.

The soba tradition

Seiunji describes itself as the birthplace of soba-kiri 蕎麦切り — cut buckwheat noodles, as opposed to earlier forms of buckwheat preparation.

According to temple tradition, Gōkai Honjō brought noodle-making technique back from Hangzhou. The claim is supported by a passage in Shiojiri 塩尻, written by Amano Sadakage, a retainer of the Owari domain.

We state this as a tradition and a documentary reference, not as established fact. The origins of soba-kiri are disputed, and other sites make competing claims.

The head priest makes the noodles himself at the temple’s soba events. A short film of this is on YouTube: watch?v=mE9PDCBvy2Y

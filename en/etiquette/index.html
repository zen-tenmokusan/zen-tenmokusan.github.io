Etiquette & what to bring

Seiunji is a working training temple, not a visitor attraction with staff to correct you. Almost nothing here is signposted in English. This page exists so that you can arrive already knowing how to behave — which is, in the end, the most comfortable way to arrive.

The short version

  1. Bring cash in small notes and coins. There is no card payment anywhere at the temple.
  2. Bring one more warm layer than you think you need. The temple is at 1,050 m.
  3. Wear shoes you can walk on uneven granite in, and take them off indoors.
  4. Phone on silent. Always, but especially during sitting.
  5. For an overnight stay, bring a sleeping bag — or decide in advance to pay ¥2,000 for futon rental.

What to wear

For walking the Zen garden
The garden is a slope of granite boulders, not a paved path. Wear shoes with grip and a closed toe. After rain the rock is slippery. Sandals are a poor idea.

For zazen 坐禅

  • Loose trousers. You will sit cross-legged for up to an hour. Tight jeans and short skirts make that unpleasant.
  • Plain, quiet clothing. No dress code is enforced, but the room is silent and still, and bright or noisy clothing draws attention you probably do not want.
  • Warm layers you can remove. Rock zazen is outdoors. Sitting still outdoors is much colder than walking outdoors.
  • No strong perfume or scented products. In a hall where everyone is sitting close and breathing slowly, scent carries.
  • Socks. Indoor practice means bare floors and no shoes.

What to bring

For a day visit

  • Cash — ¥300 for the garden, plus coins for goshuin (¥300 each)
  • Water and food (there are no shops at the bus stop)
  • Rain layer — mountain weather changes fast
  • Insect repellent in summer

For a retreat or overnight stay, add

  • Sleeping bag (or ¥2,000 for futon rental)
  • Towel — and a second one if you plan to use Tenmokuzan Onsen (¥600)
  • Toiletries
  • Head torch — the grounds are unlit at night
  • Indoor socks
  • Any medication you need — there is no pharmacy nearby
  • A book is fine; a laptop is fine (there is Wi-Fi and power) — but read the room

Leave at home

  • Drones
  • Loud speakers or music players
  • Expectations of a concierge

How to behave

These are the customs of Japanese temples generally, and they are what is expected here.

Entering

  • Remove your shoes before stepping onto any raised wooden floor. Line them up facing out.
  • A small bow at the gate is customary. Nobody will check.

In the halls

  • Speak quietly or not at all.
  • Do not touch statues, altar objects or ritual implements.
  • Do not step over cushions, and do not sit on anything that is not a seat.

During zazen

  • Arrive before it begins. Entering a silent hall late is the one thing that genuinely disturbs others.
  • Once sitting starts, stay still and stay quiet. If you need to shift position, do it slowly.
  • If you cannot sit cross-legged, say so beforehand — a chair or kneeling posture can be arranged. Struggling silently is not a virtue here.
  • You will not be hit with a stick unless you ask for it and it is offered.

Photography

For now: if you intend to photograph anything beyond the garden, ask the temple in advance. Do not photograph people sitting.

The cemetery
Seiunji is an active cemetery and the memorial ground of the Takeda clan. Families visit their own graves here. Walk through quietly, and do not photograph graves.

Paying
The garden fee is collected by an honour box. There is no attendant, no ticket and no receipt. Put ¥300 in per person. This system works because people use it honestly.

Practical realities at 1,050 metres

  • Cold. Colder than Kōfu, much colder than Tokyo, at every hour of every season.
  • Weather turns quickly. Rock zazen moves indoors in bad weather; the decision is made on the day.
  • No convenience store, no ATM, no restaurant at the bus stop.
  • Mobile signal
  • The last bus is early. Confirm it on the day you arrive, before you settle in. See Getting here.

Language

The temple operates in Japanese. Written English by email is the most reliable way to communicate.

Simple, short sentences in English work better than long polite ones. If you use a translation app, a short Japanese message alongside your English is appreciated.

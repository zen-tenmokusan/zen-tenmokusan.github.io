Staying overnight

You can sleep here. You should know exactly what that means before you book, because it is not a hotel and it is not a ryokan.

Read this first

Temple lodging at Seiunji is communal floor sleeping in a shared room, separated by gender. There are no private rooms inside the temple. You bring your own sleeping bag.

We state this plainly, at the top of the page, on purpose. Guests who arrive expecting a private room with bedding are disappointed, and that disappointment is entirely avoidable.

The discomfort is not an oversight or a cost-saving measure. Sleeping on the floor in a shared room, waking before dawn for the morning service, and cleaning the floors before breakfast are how training temples have operated for centuries. The inconvenience is part of the practice, not a lapse in hospitality. If that is not the night you want, the alternatives below are real and we will help you arrange one.

Temple lodging is used by participants in the two-day Genjū retreats 幻住坐禅会. See Zazen & Zen practice for retreat dates and fees.

Option 2 — Private room at the guesthouse by the gate

If a shared room is not workable for you, a guesthouse (minshuku) at the temple gate offers a private room to retreat participants at a special rate:

  • ¥4,500 per person, room only (no meals) — sudomari 素泊まり

Tell the temple when you apply that you would prefer a private room, and this can be arranged.

The condition of staying

Temple camping is not a campsite rental. Staying overnight obliges you to join the next morning’s service (朝課) and rock zazen 岩坐禅.

That is the deal, and it is the reason the grounds are opened at all. If you do not intend to sit the following morning, please book a campsite elsewhere — there is no hard feeling in that, but we would rather say it now than at 6 a.m.

What to bring for any overnight stay

  • Sleeping bag (or pay ¥2,000 for futon rental — decide before you arrive)
  • Warm layers. At 1,050 m the night is cold in every season, including August.
  • Towel — you will want one if you use the hot spring
  • Head torch — the grounds are not lit at night
  • Cash — there is no card payment at the temple and no ATM nearby
  • Indoor socks — floors are wooden and shoes come off

What the night is actually like

Lights out is early because the morning is early. The building is old — the kuri 庫裏 dates from 1592 — and old buildings are not soundproof. You will hear wind, rain on the roof, other people breathing, and in the morning a bell.

People come back. Returning participants receive a Tenmokusan towel, which is the temple’s small way of saying it noticed.
